## Where it comes from

Latin, and it is doing more grammatical work than it looks like. **Vice** is the ablative of *vicis*, meaning a turn or a change—the same *vice* that gives you a **vice president**, the person standing in the place of someone else. **Versa** is the feminine past participle of *vertere*, to turn. Put together: the position having been turned.

So *vice versa* means the positions are swapped. English borrowed the whole phrase intact around 1600 and never bothered to translate it, which is why it still looks foreign and gets handled like something breakable.

## Why the wrong one survives

Because of how it sounds. Most people say it with three syllables—*VY-suh VER-suh*—and that middle schwa lands exactly where the English article **a** would go. Write down what you hear and you get *vice-a versa*, which looks almost plausible if you have never seen the phrase in print.

The other two are eye-slips. **Visa** and **vise** are both common words that sit one letter away, so autocorrect leaves them alone and your eye skates right over them.

And *versa vice* is just the phrase getting turned around, which is at least thematically consistent.

## In fairness

Both pronunciations are correct. *VY-suh VER-suh* and the flatter two-syllable *vice versa* are equally standard, and dictionaries list both without comment. Say it however you like—just don't spell the extra syllable.

The hyphenated *vice-versa* turns up in older British printing and is not exactly an error, but it is out of fashion everywhere now. Two words, no hyphen.
